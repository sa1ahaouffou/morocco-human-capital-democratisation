# Is Morocco's human capital a binding constraint on democratisation?
### An empirical gap analysis

*A. Salaheddine — September 2026*
*Data: World Bank WDI & Human Capital Index, Barro-Lee v3 (2021), V-Dem v15, Maddison Project 2023, HCP RGPH 2024*

---

## 0. The question, and the short answer

The hypothesis under test is the one that prompted this exercise: **Morocco's level of
education is so low that a full democratic transition — a genuine parliamentary monarchy
with an executive accountable to parliament — is not feasible, in contrast to Korea,
Taiwan or Vietnam.**

The data do not support that hypothesis as stated, but they support a sharper and more
uncomfortable version of it. Four findings:

1. **Income is not the constraint.** Morocco today is richer than **68 %** of all
   countries at the moment they democratised (120 transitions with income data,
   1960–2025).
2. **Mean schooling is not the constraint either.** At 6.2 years, Morocco sits at the
   **46th percentile** of schooling at transition (N = 127). The median transition happened at
   6.3 years. Spain democratised at 5.9 years, Portugal at 3.5, Brazil at 3.8,
   Indonesia at 5.0 — all below Morocco today, and Spain and Portugal consolidated.
3. **The distribution is the constraint.** Morocco's *mean* is ordinary; its *dispersion*
   is extreme. **34 % of Moroccan adults aged 25+ have never been to school at all**
   (42 % of those aged 40–64; 55 % of women in 2015). That puts Morocco at the **72nd
   percentile** of the unschooled-mass distribution at transition: fewer than three
   transitions in ten occurred in a society with a larger uneducated adult population.
   Morocco's education Gini of **0.56** is the second highest in a 25-country comparator
   set, behind only Senegal.
4. **But Morocco does not have one electorate — it has two.** Disaggregating by age
   (Wittgenstein Centre, 2025): Moroccans aged **40–64** have 4.9 years of schooling and
   42 % unschooled — the 78th percentile of the transition distribution. Moroccans aged
   **20–39** have **9.1 years** and **10 %** unschooled — the **76th percentile from the
   top**, i.e. better schooled than three-quarters of all countries at the moment they
   democratised, and level with South Korea in 1988 and Poland in 1990. The national
   deficit is a legacy stock held by cohorts that demographic replacement is retiring.
5. **Morocco is already less democratic than its human capital predicts.** Regressing the
   V-Dem electoral democracy index on log income and schooling across 151 countries,
   Morocco's predicted score is **0.425**; its actual score is **0.26**. The gap of
   **−0.17** means the binding constraint is not the education stock — it is the
   institutional design that keeps the effective chief executive outside electoral
   competition.

The honest summary is therefore: **Morocco's education deficit does not make a transition
impossible; it makes a *good* transition harder, and it is not what is currently
preventing one.** The Korea/Taiwan comparison that motivated the doubt is the wrong
benchmark — those are the top decile of the historical distribution, not the threshold.

Two entries in this table do most of the work. The first is the **42 % with no schooling**,
which is what the mean of 6.2 years conceals. The second is the **closed-autocracy
classification**, which is not a judgement about election quality: under the Regimes of the
World rule (Lührmann, Tannenberg & Lindberg 2018), a country is a closed autocracy when
citizens cannot choose *either* the chief executive *or* the legislature in multiparty
elections. Morocco elects its legislature. It is coded closed because the effective chief
executive — the King — is not subject to election. That single institutional fact, not the
literacy rate, is what places Morocco in the same regime category as Saudi Arabia and the
UAE while Algeria, Egypt and Iran are graded one step higher.

## 2. Finding 1 — income is not the constraint (Figures 1a, 2, 3a)

Across the 144 democratic transitions recorded by V-Dem since 1960, the **median** country
democratised at a GDP per capita of **$5,537** (Maddison, 2011 int$). Morocco's is
**$8,400**. Two-thirds of all transitions in the modern record occurred in countries poorer
than Morocco is now.

This matches, rather than contradicts, the econometric literature. Przeworski & Limongi's
(1997) central result is that income does *not* raise the probability of a transition to
democracy; it raises the probability that a democracy, once established, survives. Boix &
Stokes (2003) reversed part of that conclusion for the pre-1950 period, but no one in this
literature argues for an income floor anywhere near Morocco's level. India democratised at
roughly $1,000; Benin at $1,790; Mali at $1,115.

**Conclusion:** the modernisation literature gives no basis for an income-based objection
to Moroccan democratisation. Whatever the constraint is, it is not GDP per capita.

## 3. Finding 2 — mean schooling is not the constraint either (Figures 2, 3b)

The median country at the moment of transition had **6.3 years** of adult schooling.
Morocco has **6.2** (UNDP) or **6.3** (HCP's own 2024 census figure). Morocco is at the
46th percentile — almost exactly the historical median.

The relevant comparisons are not Korea and Taiwan. They are:

Spain in 1978 is the single most instructive case: a Mediterranean society emerging from a
long personalist authoritarian regime, with **5.9 years** of adult schooling and **a third
of its adults with no schooling at all** — and it produced a consolidated liberal
democracy. Korea and Taiwan were the *most* educated countries in the entire transition
distribution, at roughly the 85th–90th percentile. Benchmarking Morocco against them and
concluding "we are not ready" is benchmarking against the top decile.

## 4. Finding 3 — what *is* unusual: the distribution, not the mean (Figures 3c, 5, 8, 9)

This is where the intuition behind the original question turns out to be right, but for a
different reason than expected.

The median transition occurred with **13.7 %** of adults unschooled (Barro-Lee basis) or
**17.2 %** (Wittgenstein basis). Morocco's figure was **42.4 %** on Barro-Lee's 2015
snapshot — the 81st percentile — and is **34.5 %** on the Wittgenstein Centre's 2025
estimate, the **72nd percentile**. Either way Morocco sits in the upper tercile, closer to
Türkiye 1966 (60 %) and Ghana 1980 (63 %) than to Spain (32 %) or Portugal (27 %), but the
extreme reading was partly a vintage artefact. §4b works through the reconciliation.

The dispersion is overwhelmingly a **gender** phenomenon: **54.9 %** of Moroccan women
aged 25–64 never attended school, against 29.2 % of men — a 25.7-point gap, the second
largest in the comparator set after Senegal. It compounds with a female labour-force
participation rate of **19.7 %**, among the lowest in the world outside the Gulf and
Afghanistan. A democratic electorate in which a majority of adult women have never been
to school and four in five are outside the formal labour market is a materially different
object from the one that emerged in Seoul in 1987 or Madrid in 1978.

Summarising the whole attainment distribution in one number, Morocco's **education Gini is
0.556** — above India (0.44), Egypt (0.44), Algeria (0.41) and Tunisia (0.38), and roughly
**four and a half times** Korea's (0.12) or Taiwan's (0.11).

This distinction is not cosmetic. Castelló-Climent (2008) shows precisely that the mean
years of schooling is the wrong statistic: what predicts the implementation and durability
of democracy is the education of the *majority* of society, not the average, because the
average can be driven by a small, highly educated elite sitting above a large unschooled
mass. Morocco is close to a textbook case of that configuration: 7.7 % of adults have
completed tertiary education while 42 % have no schooling whatever. Barro (1999) makes
the complementary point from the other side — his estimated propensity for democracy rises
with primary schooling *and* with a smaller male–female gap in primary attainment. Morocco
scores badly on exactly those two margins while scoring unremarkably on the mean.

## 4b. Is this an artefact of old data? No — but age matters more than vintage (Figures 11, 12)

Barro-Lee v3 ends in **2015**, and it carries the two statistics doing the most work above.
That is a real vulnerability, so I re-ran the exercise on independent sources.

**The sources agree.** Across 141 countries in 2015, Barro-Lee and the Wittgenstein Centre's
independent reconstruction correlate at **r = 0.96** on the unschooled share (mean absolute
difference 3.4 points) and **r = 0.95** on mean years (0.83 years). On Morocco's mean years
today every source converges: Wittgenstein **6.0** (2025), UNDP **6.2** (2023), HCP's own
census **6.3** (2024). UNESCO's UIS gives 4.9 but its latest Moroccan observation is **2017**
— for Morocco specifically, UIS is *less* current than Barro-Lee is stale.

| Source | Ages | Year | Mean years | % no schooling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Barro-Lee v3 | 25–64 | 2015 | 5.11 | 42.4 |
| UNESCO UIS (Feb-2026 release) | 25+ | 2017 | 4.94 | — |
| Wittgenstein Centre v3.3 | 25+ | 2015 | 4.64 | 46.1 |
| Wittgenstein Centre v3.3 | 25+ | 2020 | 5.26 | 40.4 |
| Wittgenstein Centre v3.3 | 25+ | 2025 | **6.02** | **34.5** |
| UNDP HDR | 25+ | 2023 | 6.20 | — |
| **HCP, RGPH 2024** | 25+ | 2024 | **6.30** | — |

**The benchmark survives the swap.** Re-running the whole transition exercise on
Wittgenstein data — every country, every transition year, one consistent source — moves
Morocco's mean-years percentile from 45.7 to **44.2** (no change) and its unschooled-share
percentile from 81.2 to **71.7**. The headline conclusions are unaffected; only the
"near the historical extreme" phrasing needed softening to "upper tercile."

**What the new data adds is the age dimension, and it changes the picture materially.**
Barro-Lee's 25–64 aggregate hides a bimodal society:

| Moroccan cohort, 2025 | % no schooling | percentile | Mean years | percentile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aged 40–64 | 42.5 | 78th | 4.92 | 30th |
| All adults 25+ | 34.5 | 72nd | 6.02 | 44th |
| **Aged 20–39** | **10.4** | **38th** | **9.09** | **76th** |

Moroccans under 40 are **better schooled than three-quarters of all countries at the moment
they democratised**. At 9.1 years they sit between South Korea in 1988 (9.4) and Poland in
1990 (9.8) — the cases invoked as the unreachable benchmark. Moroccans over 40 sit near
Türkiye 1990.

This matters for the original question in a specific way. The human-capital objection to
democratisation is implicitly an objection about the *median voter*. Morocco's median voter
is getting younger and much better schooled every year, mechanically, without any policy
change: the 60+ cohort is 63 % unschooled, the 20–39 cohort 10 %. Whatever bite the
education constraint has is **shrinking fast and will keep shrinking**, which is the
opposite of a structural barrier.

The counterweight, and it is a serious one: the under-40 cohort's **years** are East Asian,
but its **learning** is not (§5), and Morocco's under-40 women remain far outside the labour
market. Quantity has converged; quality and participation have not.

## 5. Finding 4 — quality: Morocco loses four years of schooling to weak learning (Figure 4)

Years of schooling overstate Moroccan human capital more than they do almost anywhere else
in the comparison set. A Moroccan child entering school today can expect **10.4 years** of
schooling but only **6.3 learning-adjusted years** — a loss of **4.1 years**. The
corresponding losses are 2.2 years in Vietnam, 1.9 in Korea, 2.5 in Spain.

Morocco's harmonized test score of **380** sits below Tunisia (384), Indonesia (395), India
(399) and even Senegal (412) — Senegal, with less than three years of mean schooling, gets
more learning out of each year in class than Morocco does. PISA 2022 confirms this
independently: Morocco ranked 71st of 81 in mathematics, **79th of 81 in reading**, and
76th in science, with reading down twenty points from 2018.

So the flow is not closing the gap on the quality margin either. If the concern is whether
the Moroccan electorate of 2040 will be equipped for high-quality democratic politics, the
learning gap — not the enrolment rate, which is already near 90 % at secondary level — is
where the problem is located.

---

## 6. Finding 5 — East Asia is the wrong benchmark, and it teaches sequencing, not thresholds (Figure 7)

Korea and Taiwan are informative for a different reason than usually assumed. They are
cases of **sequencing**: both built near-universal mass schooling *under authoritarian
rule* and democratised only afterwards, arriving at the transition with 9+ years of adult
schooling and under 9 % of adults unschooled.

Morocco's adult schooling stock in 2015 (5.1 years) was where **Korea's stood in 1968**,
**Taiwan's in 1979**, **Spain's in 1973**, **Portugal's in 1985**, **Vietnam's in 1997**
and **Tunisia's in 1998** — a lag of 47, 36, 42, 30, 18 and 17 years respectively.

But note what this does and does not imply. It implies that if Morocco democratises now, it
will do so with an electorate resembling Spain's in the mid-1970s rather than Korea's in
the late 1980s. It does **not** imply that the transition is infeasible — Spain's was.

---

## 7. Finding 6 — Vietnam and Senegal break the link in both directions

Two cases in the data dispose of any mechanical reading of the modernisation hypothesis:

- **Vietnam** has an HLO of **519** — the second highest in the comparator set after
  Singapore, above Spain and Portugal — 10.7 learning-adjusted years of school, and an
  education Gini of 0.26. Its electoral democracy index is **0.16 (2024), 0.18 (2019–23 average)**, near the bottom
  of the world distribution. High-quality mass education has produced no democratisation
  whatsoever. Education is not *sufficient*.
- **Senegal** has **2.9 years** of mean adult schooling, 67 % of adults unschooled, and the
  highest education Gini in the comparator set — and an electoral democracy index of
  **0.61 (2024), 0.69 (2019–23 average)**, comfortably a functioning electoral democracy that has survived multiple
  alternations of power. Education is not *necessary* for transition either.

Any argument of the form "our education level rules out democracy" has to explain Senegal.
Any argument of the form "education will bring democracy" has to explain Vietnam.

## 8. Finding 7 — Morocco is *under*-democratic relative to its human capital (Figure 6)

This is the decisive result for the original question.

Regress the V-Dem electoral democracy index (2019–23 average) on log GDP per capita and
mean years of schooling, across 151 countries with populations above one million:

| Specification | log GDP pc | Mean yrs school | Learn-adj. yrs | Resource rents | N | R² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Income only | +0.098 (5.8) | | | | 151 | 0.20 |
| Schooling only | | +0.035 (7.0) | | | 151 | 0.21 |
| Both | +0.049 (1.3) | +0.020 (1.7) | | | 151 | 0.22 |
| Both + resource rents | +0.048 (1.5) | +0.015 (1.4) | | −0.010 (−3.8) | 151 | 0.33 |
| Income + learning-adjusted years | +0.015 (0.4) | | +0.043 (2.5) | | 143 | 0.24 |

*HC1 t-statistics in parentheses.*

Two things are worth noting. First, income and schooling are so collinear that neither
survives cleanly when both are included — but **learning-adjusted** years dominate income
outright, which is a small piece of independent evidence that education *quality* carries
the modernisation correlation better than either raw income or raw years.

Second, and more important, the residual. Morocco's **predicted** electoral democracy index
is **0.425**. Its **actual** value is **0.260**. The gap is **−0.165**, the 38th largest
democratic deficit out of 151 countries. This is not a Moroccan idiosyncrasy: the mean
residual is **−0.30** across MENA against **+0.04** in the rest of the world (Elbadawi &
Makdisi 2007). Within the Arab world Morocco is in fact one of the *less* deficient cases —
only Tunisia has a positive residual, and Iraq and Lebanon are closer to prediction.

Morocco's deficit also cannot be explained by the standard resource-curse story (Ross 2001).
Morocco's total natural-resource rents were **0.4 % of GDP** in 2021, against 22.6 % in
Algeria, 25.6 % in Saudi Arabia, 30.4 % in Iran and 43.4 % in Iraq. Morocco carries an Arab-
sized democracy deficit without the Arab world's rents. What
Morocco shares with the deficit group is a **hereditary executive**, which is exactly
Menaldo's (2012) account of MENA monarchical resilience.

**The interpretation is unambiguous: the gap between Morocco's actual and predicted
democracy is not a human-capital gap. Morocco already has the income and the schooling
associated with substantially more democracy than it has.**

## 9. Finding 8 — what education *does* predict: not the transition, but what follows

I tested whether education at the moment of transition predicts what happens next. Three
results, reported honestly including the null:

- **Ten-year survival of the new democracy: no relationship with schooling.** Overall
  survival is 64 %. By schooling tercile at transition: 62 % (low), 67 % (mid), 61 % (high).
  A logit of survival on mean years gives a coefficient of +0.015 (p = 0.82). Income is
  weakly positive (p = 0.11), consistent with Przeworski & Limongi (1997).
- **The democracy *level* attained in the decade after transition does rise with education.**
  Regressing mean electoral democracy over years T+1…T+10 on schooling at transition:
  **+0.0127 (p = 0.003)**. On the unschooled share: **−0.0017 per percentage point (p = 0.002)**.
  Both survive on their own; both are absorbed by income when entered jointly. By tercile,
  post-transition democracy averages 0.55 / 0.60 / 0.64 and the liberal-democracy index
  0.40 / 0.46 / 0.52.
- **Applied to Morocco:** its unschooled share of 42 % versus the 13.7 % median implies a
  post-transition electoral democracy index roughly **0.05 lower** than the median
  democratiser's. That is a real penalty — about one-seventh of the gap between Indonesia
  (0.50) and Chile (0.85) today — but it is three times smaller than the **0.17** deficit
  Morocco already carries for institutional reasons.

The mechanism the literature proposes for this is Glaeser, Ponzetto & Shleifer (2007):
schooling raises the returns to civic participation, so educated societies mobilise more
cheaply in defence of democracy and against coups. Murtin & Wacziarg (2014), using
1870–2000 data, find primary schooling to be a stronger trigger of democratic transition
than income. Both are consistent with what I find in the post-transition levels, and
neither implies a threshold below which transition is impossible.

## 10. The causal caveat, stated plainly

The cross-country correlations above are not causal, and the most careful work says so.
Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson & Yared (2008) show that the income–democracy correlation
disappears once country fixed effects are included; the companion paper (2005) shows the
same for education — "there is no evidence that countries that increase their education are
more likely to become democratic."

I replicated this on my own panel (five-year intervals, 1960–2015, 161 countries):

| Specification | Lagged democracy | Lagged log GDP pc | Lagged yrs school | N |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pooled OLS | +0.877 (51.3) | −0.000 (−0.0) | **+0.0068 (4.1)** | 1,506 |
| Country + period fixed effects | +0.637 (19.4) | −0.007 (−0.6) | **+0.0011 (0.2)** | 1,506 |

Schooling is a strong predictor in the cross-section and **nothing** within countries. This
matters for the policy reading in both directions: it undercuts any claim that Morocco must
first educate its way to democracy, and equally any claim that educating will
automatically deliver it. Figure 10 makes the same point with Morocco alone: between 1950
and 2015 Morocco multiplied its adult schooling **seventeenfold**, from 0.3 to 5.1 years,
while the electoral democracy index moved from 0.05 to 0.26 and the regime classification
never changed. The 2011 constitutional reform moved the index by **+0.009**. Turnout in
legislative elections was 45.5 % (2011), 42.3 % (2016) and 50.4 % (2021) — consistent with
Benchemsi's (2012) reading of 2011 as a constitutional settlement that conceded the
appearance of parliamentary government while retaining the substance of monarchical
executive power.

## 11. Bottom line: the gap analysis proper

Stating the gaps against the historical transition distribution, in order of how binding
they actually are:

| Dimension | Morocco | Benchmark (median at transition) | Gap | Binding? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GDP per capita | $8,400 | $5,537 | **+$2,863 (surplus)** | No |
| Mean years of schooling | 6.2 | 6.3 | −0.1 years | No |
| Adults with no schooling, 25+ | 34 % | 17 % | **+17 points** | **Yes — for quality** |
| …of which aged 20–39 | **10 %** | 17 % | **−7 points (surplus)** | **No — already closed** |
| …of which aged 40–64 | 42 % | 17 % | **+25 points** | Yes, but retiring |
| Women with no schooling | 55 % | — | largest in comparator set bar Senegal | **Yes — for quality** |
| Learning quality (HLO) | 380 | ~430 (comparators) | **−50 points ≈ 1.5 school-years** | **Yes — for the next generation** |
| Elected chief executive | **No** | Yes, by construction | **Categorical** | **Yes — and it is the binding one** |

The honest conclusion for the original question is a two-part one.

**On feasibility:** the empirical record gives no support to the proposition that Morocco's
education level forecloses a democratic transition. Morocco is richer than two-thirds of
all democratisers were, at the median of their schooling, and Spain — with a lower mean and
a comparable unschooled mass — produced a consolidated liberal democracy. Morocco is
currently 0.17 points *below* the democracy level its own income and education predict,
which is the opposite of a human-capital constraint.

**On quality:** the education gap is real and it predicts the *kind* of democracy that
would emerge, not whether one can emerge. A transition conducted with 33–42 % of adults
unschooled, 55 % of women unschooled, 20 % female labour-force participation, and learning
outcomes in the bottom decile of PISA would be expected — on the historical record — to
produce a democracy roughly 0.05 points lower on the electoral index than the median
democratiser, with correspondingly elevated exposure to clientelism and personalist
capture, and with women's political weight far below their demographic weight. That is a
meaningful quality penalty, not a barrier to entry.

The lever that closes the quality gap fastest is not more years — enrolment is already near
90 % at secondary level — but **learning per year** and **the female schooling deficit in
the 40+ cohorts**, which is a stock problem that only demographic replacement and adult
literacy programmes can touch. The lever that closes the *feasibility* gap is not
educational at all.

## 12. Caveats and data notes

1. **Barro-Lee v3 ends in 2015**, so the gender split and the education Gini (Figures 8–9)
   remain 2015 values. The headline unschooled share and all cohort figures have been
   re-estimated on Wittgenstein Centre v3.3 (2025) and the benchmark re-run on that source
   end to end (§4b, Figures 11–12). Wittgenstein 2025 figures are *modelled
   projections* under SSP2, not observations — though 2025 values are driven almost entirely
   by cohorts already educated, so the projection risk is small. HCP's RGPH 2024 reports
   illiteracy at 24.8 % (age 10+), a different concept from "never attended school" and not
   directly comparable.
2. **Transitions are identified from V-Dem's Regimes of the World** as the first year a
   country moves from category {0,1} to {2,3}. This is one of several defensible codings;
   an Polity-based or Boix-Miller-Rosato coding would give a somewhat different event set.
   Re-running on an alternative coding is the obvious robustness check.
3. **Morocco is coded a closed autocracy by construction**, because of the chief-executive
   rule. Readers who consider that coding too severe should note that the continuous
   electoral-democracy index — which does not use that rule — also places Morocco at 0.26,
   level with Algeria's 0.260 and below Türkiye's 0.289 and Tunisia's 0.422 (2024 values).
4. **All regressions are descriptive.** None has a credible identification strategy; the
   fixed-effects panel in §10 is included precisely to show what happens when the
   cross-sectional variation is taken away.
5. **The cohort result is about schooling years, not learning.** The 20–39 cohort's
   9.1 years are not 9.1 Korean years: Morocco's learning-adjusted discount (§5) applies to
   them too. The convergence is in quantity only.
6. **Taiwan** is absent from World Bank sources; its income comes from Maddison and its
   schooling from Barro-Lee, with no HLO available.
